## Why a chart and a timer work together

A chart answers "what comes next?" and a timer answers "how long do I have?" Young children struggle with both. The Mount Sinai Parenting Center suggests turning routines like mornings and bedtime into a simple visual chart of steps, and offering a timer so children know how much time is left before a change.

Together they take some of the nagging out of the day. The chart says it's time for shoes, and the timer says when shoes are done.

## Morning routine chart

| Step | Picture idea | Timer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Potty and wash hands | Toilet, soap | None |
| 2. Get dressed | Shirt and pants | 10 minutes |
| 3. Breakfast | Bowl and spoon | 15 to 20 minutes |
| 4. Brush teeth | Toothbrush | 2 minutes |
| 5. Shoes and coat | Shoes | 5 to 10 minutes |

Lay clothes out the night before so "get dressed" is a choice between two shirts, not a search through a drawer.

## Leaving the house chart

Getting out the door is often the hardest transition of the day, because it usually means stopping something fun.

| Step | Picture idea | Timer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Finish playing | Toy box | 5 minutes |
| 2. Toys away | Basket | 5 minutes |
| 3. Potty | Toilet | None |
| 4. Shoes and coat | Shoes | 5 to 10 minutes |
| 5. Grab your bag | Backpack | None |

Start the "finish playing" timer while your child is still playing, and show it to them: "When the color is gone, we put the blocks away." The warning comes from something they can watch, not from a sudden "we're leaving now."

## Bedtime routine chart

HealthyChildren.org, the American Academy of Pediatrics' parenting site, says children "thrive on a regular bedtime routine" and suggests starting early with a routine such as "brush, book, bed." It also recommends turning screens off at least an hour before bedtime and choosing calm activities like reading or a bath over active play.

| Step | Picture idea | Timer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Bath | Bathtub | 10 to 15 minutes |
| 2. Pajamas | Pajamas | 5 minutes |
| 3. Brush teeth | Toothbrush | 2 minutes |
| 4. Books | Book | 10 to 15 minutes |
| 5. Lights out | Moon | None |

The timer is most useful at the steps that tend to stretch. A visible end to book time turns "one more book" into a quiet look at how much color is left.

## Making your own chart

- **Use pictures.** Draw them, print clip art, or photograph your child doing each step. A photo of their own shoes beats any icon.
- **Keep the order fixed.** The same steps in the same order every day is what makes it a routine.
- **Put it at child height.** On the fridge, the bathroom door, or by the front door.
- **Let them move a marker.** A clothespin or magnet that moves down the chart gives a clear sense of progress.
- **Start with one routine.** Get mornings working before you add bedtime.

## Tips for using the timer

- **Show it first.** Say what happens when the time runs out, then start the timer together.
- **Let your child press start.** It's their timer and their routine.
- **Match timer names to the chart.** In Montessori Visual Timer you can rename a quick timer and pick its color, so "Shoes On" on the chart is "Shoes On" in the app.
- **Lock it if needed.** If little fingers keep adding time, set a PIN and turn on auto-lock so the timer locks when it starts.
- **Follow through.** When the color is gone, move to the next step, calmly and every time.
- **Adjust after a week.** If a step always runs out, give it more time. A timer that's too short teaches that the timer can't be trusted.
