## Where to put it

**Whole class.** Stand the iPad or tablet in landscape at the front of the room, at about the students' eye level, away from glare from the window. If kids sit on a rug, put it where the back row can see it, not just the front.

**One student.** For a student who needs their own timer, use a phone or small tablet on their desk. A second device means the class timer and theirs don't have to match.

**Set the PIN first.** Choose a PIN in settings before you hand the device to students. Auto-lock on start is on by default, so once a timer is running, students can see it but can't pause, reset or change it without the PIN.

**Check the screen setting.** The app keeps the screen on while a timer runs, which is on by default. Leave it on for the classroom tablet so the screen doesn't go dark halfway through an activity.

## Centers and rotations

Rotations are where a shared timer helps most. Everyone moves at the same signal, and you don't have to break off with a small group to announce it.

- **Edit a quick timer into "Centers."** Set it once and start every rotation with one tap.
- **Show it before the first rotation.** "When the color is gone, clean up your center and move to the next one."
- **Add a clean-up step.** A one-minute timer between rotations gives students time to tidy up, so they aren't rushed at the end of the main timer.
- **Keep your teacher table in view of the timer** so you can wrap up a guided group without checking a clock.

## Clean-up and transitions

A vague "clean up, please" tends to stretch. Three minutes with the color shrinking is a task students can finish.

The Cleanup quick timer is already in the app. For lining up or moving from the rug to tables, make a short one-to-two-minute timer and use it the same way every time.

## Brain breaks

Understood.org suggests using a visual timer during brain breaks "so students can see the time remaining," keeping breaks to one to five minutes, and planning them before fatigue or distraction sets in. It also suggests a calm ten-second countdown at the very end. The timer handles the length, and your voice handles the last few seconds.

## Independent work and test time

For independent work, a timer helps students pace themselves without asking you how long is left. Start with short blocks for younger children and build up.

During quiet tests or reading, the gentle alert won't jolt the room. If you'd rather it end silently, keep the device's volume down and just watch for the color to finish.

## Turn-taking

One iPad, one class computer, one popular toy. A short timer settles who goes next, and students can see that a turn is fair. Put the timer next to the shared item rather than at the front of the room.

## Timers for one student: IEP and 504 accommodations

Understood.org lists "use a visual timer to help with time management" among common classroom accommodations. It explains that accommodations change how a student learns or shows what they know, not what they're expected to learn, and that they're typically written into a student's IEP or 504 plan if they have one.

Some practical points:

- **Follow the plan.** If a student's plan names a timer, the team decides how it's used. This post isn't a substitute for that.
- **Give them their own device** if the class timer isn't close enough to see easily.
- **Let them start it.** Pressing start helps the timer feel like it belongs to the student.
- **It's a tool, not a treatment.** A timer can make time visible. It doesn't address the reasons a student needs support, so keep talking with families and specialists.

## A caution for Montessori classrooms

Montessori classrooms protect long stretches of uninterrupted work. AMI's article on the three-hour work cycle says "there is no magic in the number '3'," and that the important thing is "to never interrupt children to gather them, asking them to put their work away." It also notes that gathering a small group for a brief lesson is still fine.

So in a Montessori room, use the timer for routines around the work cycle, like snack, clean-up, turn-taking and transitions outdoors, and not to end a child's chosen work. A child can also use a timer on their own for practice, such as timing a task they've chosen.
