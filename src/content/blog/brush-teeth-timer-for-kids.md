## Toothbrushing as practical life

In Montessori education, everyday tasks like dressing, pouring and cleaning are called practical life. The American Montessori Society says "Practical Life activities are the activities of everyday life," and its home guide lists "brushing one's teeth" among them. AMS describes the child's inner plea as "Help me to do it alone!"

Toothbrushing fits well. It happens twice a day, the steps are always the same, and a young child can do most of them. The adult's job is to set up the space and then step back. The AMS guide puts it this way: "Try not to intervene or correct unless asked."

## How long and how much

The American Dental Association "recommends brushing teeth twice a day for two minutes using a fluoride toothpaste."

For toothpaste amounts, the CDC's 2019 report on children's toothpaste use says:

| Age | Amount of fluoride toothpaste |
|---|---|
| Under 3 | "A smear the size of a rice grain" |
| 3 to 6 | "No more than a pea-sized amount" |

The CDC also says to watch children younger than 6 brush, and to ask your doctor or dentist about fluoride toothpaste before age 2. The same report found that more than 38% of children aged 3 to 6 used more toothpaste than recommended, so it's worth checking what's on the brush.

## A prepared bathroom

A Montessori-style bathroom is set up so a child can do the routine without asking for help. Greenspring Montessori School's guide to the bathroom puts the goal plainly: "prepare the home environment for toddlers to be as independent as possible." Its advice includes keeping "all necessary items within reach," a step stool and a suction-cup mirror.

For toothbrushing, it suggests a tray with a toothbrush, toothpaste, "a timer, and a small water glass." It also suggests portioning toothpaste into small containers at first instead of handing over the whole tube.

What that looks like at home:

- **A sturdy step stool** so your child can reach the sink and see the mirror.
- **A mirror at their eye level**, or a small suction-cup mirror low on the wall.
- **A low shelf or tray** with their toothbrush, toothpaste and a small cup.
- **A towel on a hook** they can reach.
- **The timer where they can see it** from the stool, out of splash range.

## A step-by-step toothbrushing routine

1. **Step up.** Your child climbs onto the stool.
2. **Get ready.** Wet the toothbrush and put on the right amount of toothpaste for their age (or use one you've portioned out).
3. **Start the timer.** Your child taps Brush Teeth.
4. **Brush.** Top teeth, bottom teeth, fronts, backs and chewing surfaces, until the color is gone.
5. **Stop the alert.** Tap the screen when it plays.
6. **Spit and rinse the brush.** Put the brush back in its cup.
7. **Your turn.** For young children, finish with a quick check or a brush of your own, then let them wipe their face and hang the towel.

## When brushing is hard

Some children resist brushing whatever you try. A few things that may help:

- **Practice when it's calm.** Let your child start the timer and brush a doll's teeth first.
- **Name what's left.** "Half the color is gone. Now the bottom teeth."
- **Keep it short and predictable.** The timer ends at two minutes every time, so your child knows when it will stop.

If your child has sensory sensitivities, our guide to [visual timers for kids with ADHD or autism](/blog/visual-timer-for-adhd-autism/) covers quiet endings and transition warnings. If brushing stays a struggle, ask your child's dentist.
