"Five more minutes" is an abstract idea. A visual timer turns it into something a child can look at: a colored section that gets smaller until it's gone. Many parents of children with ADHD, and of autistic children, use one to make transitions and routines more predictable.

A timer is a tool, not a treatment. It won't change how your child's brain works, and it isn't a substitute for support from their clinician or school. What it can do is take one question, "how long is left?", off your child's plate and yours.

## Why seeing time helps some kids

Understood.org describes time blindness as "an experience, common among people with ADHD, of not having a clear sense of time." Its suggestions lean on making time visible: clocks in every room and deadlines posted where you'll see them.

For classrooms, Understood.org says "knowing there's a limit to how long an activity will last can make it easier for students with ADHD to stay engaged," and that "timers can also warn students about transitions." It adds a caution worth taking seriously at home too: "timers can make some students nervous."

The National Autistic Society says that for autistic people who find change hard, there "is not enough research" into how best to support them. Among the suggestions it collects from autistic people and professionals are a routine "that provides structure and reduces unpredictability" and to "prepare ahead and count down to any changes." A visual timer is one way to count down.

## What the research has tested

The evidence is thinner than product marketing suggests, so here is what the studies looked at.

- **Visual supports at home.** A 2020 scoping review by Rutherford and colleagues in the journal *Autism* looked at visual supports used at home and in the community. It found they "can be used successfully by children and adults on the autism spectrum," but "strong conclusions were not drawn due to the limited number and quality of the included studies." Parents in the studies described using sequence charts, timers and social stories, and asked for more support and training to keep using them.
- **Visual schedules and transitions.** A 2025 review by Mouzakes and Thompson asked whether the visual schedule itself improves transitions for autistic people. They found the included studies "provided insufficient evidence" for that, partly because schedules are usually combined with other strategies and few studies test them on their own.

We didn't find a review that tests visual timers alone. So treat a timer as one support that some children find useful, and judge it by how your own child responds.

For ADHD, the CDC lists behavior therapy, parent training in behavior management and classroom behavioral interventions among treatment options, and says "schools can be part of the treatment as well." If you're looking for treatment, start there, with your child's doctor.

## Pair the timer with a visual schedule or first-then board

A timer answers "how long?" A schedule answers "what's next?" They work best side by side.

- **First-then board.** Two pictures: the thing to do now, and what comes after. "First shoes, then park." Start a short timer for the first card and put the device next to the board.
- **Visual schedule.** A row of pictures for a routine, like the [toddler routine chart](/blog/toddler-routine-chart-visual-timer/) we use for mornings and bedtime. Not every step needs a timer. Use one for the steps that tend to stretch or stall.
- **Say what's next.** When the color runs out, point to the next picture rather than just saying "time's up."

## Transition warnings

A sudden "we're leaving now" is hard for many kids. Warnings give them time to finish.

1. **Tell them before you start the timer.** "When the color is gone, it's time to put on shoes."
2. **Use a short warning timer.** Many families like a two- or five-minute timer before a change. Understood.org's advice on screen time suggests a five-minute warning, or waiting for a natural stopping point.
3. **Check in partway.** "Look, about half is left."
4. **Keep the ending the same every time.** Same words, same next step.

## Sensory considerations

The National Autistic Society lists "unexpected noises (e.g. alarms, sirens)" among common triggers for people who are sensitive to sound. A timer alert can be one of those noises. Some ideas:

- **Make the ending silent.** In Montessori Visual Timer, turn off **Play Sound** in settings. The color still runs out, so your child sees the end without hearing it.
- **If you keep the sound, make it predictable.** Let your child hear the alert when nothing is at stake, so it isn't a surprise later. The alert keeps playing until someone taps it, which gives your child a clear job: tap to stop it.
- **Keep the screen calm.** Put the device on a plain surface away from clutter. The app has no ads, so nothing pops up mid-routine.
- **Use color to tell timers apart.** Each quick timer has its own color, so "Shoes" and "Tablet time" look different at a glance.

If any timer makes your child anxious, stop using it for a while. That's useful information, not a failure.

## Building independence

The goal is a timer your child runs, not one that runs them.

- **Let them press start.** Starting it themselves gives them some control over the countdown.
- **Name the quick timers in their words.** Edit the four quick timers to match their routines, like "Get Dressed" or "Lego Break." The app holds four, so choose the ones you use most.
- **Use the PIN lock for fairness, not control.** With a PIN set, auto-lock on start (on by default) stops anyone, sibling or parent, from quietly changing the time.
- **Keep the screen on.** "Keep Screen Awake" is on by default and only applies while a timer runs, so the clock stays visible.
- **Fade it when they're ready.** Some kids eventually need the timer only for hard transitions.

## Working with school

If a timer helps at home, tell your child's teacher. Understood.org lists "use a visual timer to help with time management" among common classroom accommodations and says accommodations are typically written into a student's IEP or 504 plan if they have one.

- **Share what works.** Which routines, how long, sound on or off, and what you say at the end.
- **Ask about consistency.** A similar setup at home and school can make the routine easier to follow.
- **Leave decisions to the team.** Whether a timer goes into a plan is up to your child's team. Teachers can find more in our [classroom visual timer guide](/blog/visual-timer-for-classroom/).
