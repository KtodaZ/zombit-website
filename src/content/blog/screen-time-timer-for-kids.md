## Why ending screen time is so hard

Shows and games are built to keep going. HealthyChildren.org points out that autoplay and notifications are "designed to keep kids engaged longer," and suggests turning them off. When one episode rolls straight into the next, there is never a natural moment to stop, so the stop comes from a parent, and it feels sudden.

Young children also can't judge time well yet. "Five more minutes" means little to a three-year-old, so the end always seems to arrive out of nowhere.

## What research says about warnings

In 2016, University of Washington researchers studied how families with children aged 1 to 5 handle the end of screen time. They interviewed 27 families, then had 28 more families keep a two-week diary.

The main findings:

- **Two-minute warnings didn't help.** Children who got a warning were more upset when the screen went away, which surprised the researchers.
- **Natural stopping points helped.** Transitions went better at the end of a video or game.
- **Routines helped.** So did ending screen time when the technology itself was the reason, such as the Wi-Fi going down.
- **Most endings went fine.** About 59% of transitions were neutral and 19% positive, while 22% were negative. Senior author Julie Kientz noted that the unpleasant few can shape how parents see the whole experience.

The study didn't test timers. The researchers suggested that a reminder coming from the technology, rather than from a parent, might go over better. As one of them put it, "Once you take that parental withholding component out of it, kids are a lot more accepting."

A visual timer fits that idea. It's on from the start, so there's no surprise countdown, and it's neutral. The timer ran out; nobody took anything away.

## A five-step screen time routine

1. **Agree before you start.** "You can watch until the timer runs out, then it's snack time." Say it while the screen is still off.
2. **Match the timer to the show.** If an episode is about 20 minutes, set 20 minutes, so the show and the timer end together at a natural stopping point.
3. **Put the timer where they can see it.** Next to the TV, or on a phone or iPad propped beside the screen. Start it where your child can see it.
4. **Turn off autoplay.** Then the screen stops on its own and agrees with the timer.
5. **Have the next thing ready.** Snack on the table, shoes by the door, a favorite toy out. It's easier to move toward something than away from something.

When the timer ends, keep it short and calm: "The color's gone. Screen time is done. Let's go have a snack." Then do it the same way tomorrow.

## Common snags

- **"Just one more!"** Calmly point to the timer. The answer is the same every day, which is what makes it work.
- **Little fingers adding time.** Set a PIN in Montessori Visual Timer and turn on auto-lock, so the timer locks itself when it starts. It stays locked after the timer ends, so an adult enters the PIN before the next one.
- **The show is longer than the limit.** Pick shorter episodes, or agree to pause at the end of a scene.
- **Big feelings anyway.** Some upset is normal. Stay close, name the feeling, and follow through. The Mount Sinai Parenting Center suggests getting up close and making eye contact with your child before a change, rather than calling across the room.

If tantrums are frequent or intense, talk with your child's pediatrician. A timer is a practical tool, not a treatment.

## Make it part of a family plan

The American Academy of Pediatrics encourages families to make a family media plan: decide on rules that fit your routines and values, talk them through with your children, and set screen-free zones such as the dinner table and the time before bed. HealthyChildren.org also recommends turning screens off at least an hour before bedtime and keeping them out of bedrooms.

A screen time timer is one small piece of that plan, the part that makes the agreed limit easy to see.
